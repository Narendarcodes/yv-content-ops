1. Executive Summary 
Aaryajanani is an NGO with an active social-media presence, including Instagram and YouTube. Its current 
social-media operation is handled by a small team of four people whose combined workload is closer to the 
work normally distributed across a larger team. 
The core problem is not simply that WhatsApp, Google Sheets, or Google Drive are imperfect tools. The deeper 
problem is that the lifecycle and memory of a content project are fragmented across multiple tools, 
conversations, files, and individual memory. There is no single persistent, connected record that reliably 
represents what a project is, how it evolved, what was decided, what was approved, what was published, and 
how it performed. 
As a result, people repeatedly perform manual coordination and reconstruction work: asking for task status, 
finding files, determining which revision was approved, remembering past experiments, locating final assets, 
and reconnecting a published post with the project that produced it.

5. Current End-to-End Workflow 
The observed workflow for a new video concept is approximately: 
1. The team meets, often through Google Meet, to brainstorm a new video concept. 
2. The discussion is collaborative; the concept is not treated as a one-person approval decision. The team 
discusses it and agrees to work on it. 
3. The concept is noted in a Google Sheet. 
4. Required inputs for the editor/designer are discussed with B, often orally. 
5. C or D must separately ask B when the required inputs will be delivered. There is currently no reliable 
tracking of that dependency. 
6. Once inputs are received, C or D works on the content. 
7. The first draft is posted in the group, historically via WhatsApp, and the Sheet is manually updated to 
indicate 'First Draft Delivered.' 
8. A and B review the draft and comment on it. 
9. A, B, and the responsible editor discuss the feedback. The editor can challenge feedback or explain why a 
review point may be incorrect. The process is collaborative rather than purely top-down. 
10. The team reaches a conclusion on the changes. 
11. The editor works on revisions and manually updates the Sheet to indicate that changes are in progress. 
12. The revised work is submitted again for review and discussion.

7. Core Problem Statement 
Aaryajanani's content team does not have a single, persistent, connected record of a content project from 
concept through production, review, completion, publication, and performance. 
Instead, project information is fragmented across meetings, spreadsheets, chat conversations, file storage, and 
individual memory. The team therefore has to perform repeated manual coordination and reconstruction work 
to answer basic operational questions such as: What is this project? Who is responsible? What version are we 
on? What feedback was given? What was approved? Where is the final file? When is it being published? What 
was actually published? And how did it perform? 
The resulting inefficiency is not merely a lack of convenience. It creates uncertainty at critical transition points 
in the content lifecycle and makes the organization's project history difficult to preserve, retrieve, and 
understand.

9. Serious Real-World Scenarios 
Scenario 1 — Wrong/uncertain version at publishing time 
Project 1 went through multiple revisions. C used a naming convention such as projectName_versionNo. C sent 
Version 3; B reviewed it and indicated that it looked good. Later C sent another version. A commented 
elsewhere. The project discussion and files were distributed across WhatsApp. No durable record clearly stated 
'Version 3 = B approved; A pending' or which later version superseded it. When the scheduled publishing time 
arrived, B could not confidently determine which version was approved. The relevant file and comments were 
also difficult to recover from WhatsApp. 
Operational consequence: publishing became uncertain at the exact moment the content needed to be 
released. 
Scenario 2 — Lead must manually interrogate the team for status 
A needed to know what C and D had completed, what they were currently working on, and what tasks had been 
initiated. The Google Sheet could not reliably answer these questions because team members were not 
consistently maintaining it. A therefore had to call C/D and ask them directly.

Operational consequence: the lead spends time reconstructing team status instead of obtaining it directly from

Scenario 3 — Experiment proposal is forgotten 
The entire team discussed creating a new type of video as an experiment. Later, C asked A and B about the 
experiment. Neither remembered the proposal. C had to explain the experiment again. 
Operational consequence: organizational knowledge created collaboratively did not remain reliably available

Scenario 4 — Scheduler cannot locate final asset or owner 
On the scheduled publishing date, the scheduler needs the completed video from Google Drive. When the file 
cannot be found, the scheduler asks the editors. There has also been a case where the scheduler forgot who 
had worked on a project and had to ask the group who created it and where the file was. 
Operational consequence: publishing depends on human memory and ad-hoc group queries. 
10. Root Causes and Contributing Factors 
The following are problem hypotheses grounded in the observed workflow. They are intentionally separated 
from product features. 
• Multiple systems contain different pieces of the same project's state. 
• The system of record for a project is not clearly defined. 
• Manual data-entry steps are disconnected from the actual work, so maintaining status feels like 
administrative overhead. 
• Important decisions are made in conversation but are not consistently converted into durable project state. 
• File storage is organized as storage, not as a connected representation of project relationships. 
• Dependencies such as 'B must deliver inputs to C' are communicated but not persistently tracked. 
• Review is collaborative and iterative, but the final relationship between comments, revisions, and approved 
versions is not explicitly preserved. 
• Publishing is operationally dependent on finding the right final artifact, but the final artifact is not reliably 
connected to the project and its schedule. 
• Post-publication information is disconnected from the originating project, making the content lifecycle 
incomplete. 
• The team is small relative to its workload, increasing the cost of every manual coordination step. 
11. What the Problem Is NOT 
• It is not simply 'WhatsApp is bad.' WhatsApp is useful for fast discussion and collaborative review. 
• It is not simply 'Google Sheets is bad.' The Sheet becomes problematic because it requires separate manual 
maintenance and does not reliably represent the full project state. 
• It is not simply 'Google Drive is bad.' Even a perfectly organized Drive would not solve fragmented 
discussions, approvals, scheduling, and performance history. 
• It is not primarily an approval-hierarchy problem. A/B/C can openly discuss feedback; A generally has higher 
final authority, but review is collaborative.

• It is not merely a lack-of-people problem. The team is indeed overloaded, but the observed failures are

12. Required Organizational Memory (Problem Definition, Not 
Solution) 
From the discovery discussion, the team considers the following information especially important to preserve 
for a specific content project: 
• The concept that was discussed and agreed upon. 
• The fact that the concept became a real video/project. 
• The assigned work and project lifecycle. 
• Changes and revisions made during production. 
• Comments, feedback, and review history. 
• The final/published video or social-media post link. 
• Performance of the published concept/video. 
The identity of the individual responsible for the work is useful operational information, but in the discovery 
discussion it was explicitly judged not to be the most critical long-term piece of information. The 
project/concept, published content, and performance were considered more important to preserve. This does 
not mean ownership is unimportant during active work. 
13. Project Lifecycle as Observed 
The emerging mental model is similar to a persistent GitHub issue: a project/concept should remain identifiable 
as one continuous unit while its state changes.

The same persistent project record is expected conceptually to support different kinds of work/events,

15. Operational Consequences 
• Time is lost to asking people for information that should be readily available. 
• Publishing can become risky because the correct final version is not immediately identifiable. 
• Managers cannot reliably see team workload and current work without manual follow-up. 
• Review cycles become harder to reconstruct when multiple revisions occur. 
• Past decisions and experiments can be forgotten. 
• New or less-informed team members have difficulty understanding historical context. 
• Files become detached from the project context that explains what they are. 
• The organization cannot easily connect content production to published outcomes and performance. 
• Operational knowledge remains partly dependent on individual memory.

17. Problem Discovery Conclusion 
The strongest evidence points to a coordination and project-memory problem caused by fragmented 
information, not to a single bad tool. 
Aaryajanani's team has a collaborative content-production process, but the process crosses several 
disconnected systems. The team's actual work, discussions, decisions, files, status, scheduling, publication, 
and performance are not represented as one continuous project history. This forces people to manually bridge 
gaps between systems and repeatedly reconstruct state. 
The central problem to preserve for future product work is therefore: 
“The team can create and discuss content collaboratively, but the organization cannot reliably preserve 
and retrieve the complete connected history and current state of that content from concept to 
performance.” 
18. Discovery Artifact: One-Line Problem Map 
Fragmented information → manual coordination → uncertain project state → file/version confusion → publishing 
friction → lost decisions/experiments → disconnected publication/performance → repeated reconstruction work. 
19. Explicit Boundary Before Solution Design 
No final solution has been selected in this document. The GitHub-style issue/repository analogy is recorded as 
the user's mental model for persistent project history, not as a committed implementation decision. The next 
product-design phase should test whether that model actually fits all observed workflows before defining 
screens, features, database schemas, or architecture.
